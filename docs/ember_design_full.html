05

What the genre already knows

Nine shipped apps, and the one number they all disagree with you about.

Your 50 m is tighter than anything that has shipped, and tighter than both platforms recommend. That is survivable, but only if you stop thinking of it as a geofence and add a dwell timer. Asking for "even closer" is the one part of the original brief the evidence says to drop.

App or guidanceRadius
Ingress action range40 mShown as a gold ring around your avatar
Pokémon GO stops and gyms80 mWas 40 m; reverting it triggered an organised boycott on accessibility and rural grounds, and 80 m was made permanent
Munzee virtual~90 m
Adventure Lab default100 mGeocaching HQ's own recommendation
Android geofencing docs100–150 mStated minimum. Also: "assume Wi-Fi location accuracy is about 50 metres"
Turf zone~25 mThe exception, and the model to copy — gated by an 18–30 second dwell timer rather than by radius alone

Real-world GPS is roughly 5 m under open sky but 7–13 m in a street with tall buildings, with occasional errors in the hundreds of metres when the sky view is blocked. At a 50 m radius in a city, a stationary phone will drift in and out of range repeatedly. Android's documentation names "your geofence is too small" as a leading cause of missed alerts.

The resolution is architectural, and it is already what Ember does. Do not register operating-system geofences: they are unreliable below 100 m, capped at roughly twenty regions on iOS, and can take minutes to fire. Instead poll position in the foreground, discard any fix with poor accuracy, and require the player to stay inside the radius for ten to twenty seconds before it ignites. That dwell requirement does four jobs at once: it filters out drive-bys, it makes location spoofing cost real time, it gives the GPS a window to settle, and it turns the unlock into a moment of tension rather than an instant.

Six mechanics worth stealing

The dwell ring · Turf

Hold position and a ring fills. Approach screen 02 already shows the ring; make it fill over about fifteen seconds rather than ignite on contact.

Range ring on the avatar · Ingress

Draw the true 50 m radius around the player, not the target. It converts "am I close enough" from a guess into a shape you can walk into.

Inverse reward · Turf

Remote places pay a large one-off; dense city places pay small but often. Two lines of arithmetic, and it is the only published fix for a map that is empty outside the centre.

A bounded grid · Geocaching

Eighty-one cells with visibly empty corners provably drives travel; an unbounded "places visited" counter does not. The tray in screen 04 is already the right shape.

Change form, not just colour · StreetComplete

Their solved markers vanish outright, which is the clearest state change in the survey. Ember keeps its markers but makes locked ones hollow outlines and lit ones solid and glowing, so the difference survives colour blindness.

Reach day seven

A seven-day streak roughly doubles the odds of returning. Below three days it is worth almost nothing, so if you build streaks at all, build exactly two forgiveness tokens and aim everything at the first week.

Three things that will bite

  • Do not build your world from Google Places. Its terms let you store a place identifier indefinitely but cache coordinates for only thirty days, which is incompatible with a game whose entire world is a list of coordinates. Curate your own list or take it from OpenStreetMap. Niantic moved Pokémon GO's base map to OpenStreetMap for related reasons.
  • The battery villain is not GPS. Instrumented teardowns of Pokémon GO put location at four to seven percent of energy, with the screen and camera dominating. The practical conclusions are to never build an augmented-reality mode, and to offer a screen-dimmed walking mode, which bought that game between twenty-three and forty percent.
  • Choose places carefully, and publish a way to remove one. The incumbent settled a nuisance suit for four million dollars and now must remove any marker within forty metres of a home within five working days. Ember is small and off-store, but a marker outside someone's house is the same problem at any scale, and a removal contact costs nothing to add now.

Architecture

Sized for the app that exists, with the two seams that let it grow.

Google publishes an official Flutter architecture guide, and it recommends MVVM: views, view models, repositories, services, with Result objects at the boundaries. That guide is written for a team app with a real backend. Ember has one asset file, one GPS stream and three tabs, so the honest version keeps the guide's shape and drops its ceremony. The layering below is the full guide minus the layers that would currently hold nothing.

Dependencies point down only. The red band is the game itself and it has no imports from Flutter, geolocator or Firebase, which is what makes the whole thing testable from a desk.

Folder layout

Not pure feature-first. Google's own reference app uses a hybrid that is worth copying exactly: the UI is organised by feature, because each screen owns one view model, while the data layer is organised by type, because repositories are shared across screens. Filing LocationService under the map feature would be a lie the moment the tray needs it.

State management

Use provider with plain ChangeNotifier view models, which is exactly what Google's reference app ships and the only combination the Flutter team endorses in writing. It needs no code generation and no new vocabulary.

The important part is that this choice barely matters. Riverpod's Notifier and Bloc's Cubit drop into the identical architectural hole, so if you already know one, use it and change nothing else. What is load-bearing is the split: the view model holds the logic, the repository is the source of truth, and the game rules are pure functions underneath both. Swapping the notification mechanism later touches one class per screen. Swapping the layering touches everything.

Two patterns from the official guide are worth taking even at this size. Result, a sealed Ok/Error pair returned across every repository boundary, so a failed GPS read or a missing asset has to be handled at the call site instead of throwing through three layers. And Command, which wraps an action and exposes running, error and completed while refusing to run twice concurrently. That re-entrancy guard is not academic here: two GPS fixes can arrive while an unlock is still writing, and without the guard the same place ignites twice.

The one flow that matters

Step 6 persists before it celebrates. If the app is killed mid-animation the place is still yours, and the reverse order would let a crash eat a reward the player physically walked for.

The two seams worth building

Every other abstraction in this app would be speculative. These two pay for themselves immediately.

Seam 1 · LocationService

You cannot develop this game by walking outside every time. A FakeLocationService replaying a list of coordinates drives the entire app from a unit test or a debug menu, at any speed. This single interface is the difference between a testable game and a two-week manual QA loop.

Seam 2 · The two repositories

Destinations come from a bundled JSON today and Firestore later. Unlocks live in shared_preferences today and sync to an account later. Both swaps are one line in main.dart if the interface exists, and a refactor across every screen if it does not.

The trap that will bite you

Never rebuild the marker set on a position update. google_maps_flutter takes markers declaratively as a Set<Marker> and re-diffs the entire set whenever any part of it changes. Changing one marker among three thousand costs the same as changing all of them, and a cold load of that many has been measured freezing the UI for over a second.

Ember hits this case head-on, because GPS fires every ten metres and the naive implementation rebuilds markers in the same handler. The fix is to keep the marker set identity-stable and rebuild it only when an unlock actually changes a marker's colour, which is a few dozen times ever. The live distance, the warming tint and the proximity ring are ordinary widgets painted over the map, not marker properties. Build the two marker images once at startup and hold them in a map, rather than making one per pin. Keep the visible set in the low hundreds, and this never becomes a problem.

And the expensive one: never set a map ID. Displaying a Google map on a phone is genuinely free and unlimited, on a no-cost billing line. But the moment a map is created with a map identifier, every single map open reclassifies as a billable dynamic-map load, charged after a small monthly allowance.

Two features quietly require one: the newer advanced-marker type, and cloud-hosted map styling. So Ember uses classic markers, and applies its dark map theme from a style string inside the app rather than from a cloud style. Same look, no map identifier, no bill. This is one line of code separating free from expensive, and it is worth writing down before somebody adopts advanced markers for the collision behaviour.

Testing without going outside

The reason LocationService is abstract is that geolocator ships no fake, and a game you can only test by walking is a game you will not test. Behind the interface, a fake backed by a StreamController lets you emit any coordinate on demand, so the whole rule set becomes a desk test:

// the whole game, verified at a desk
test('ignites once, at 50 m, and never twice', () async {
  final gps = FakeLocationService();
  final game = GameController(location: gps, ...);

  gps.emit(lat, lng, accuracy: 8);   // 340 m away  → nothing
  gps.emit(lat, lng, accuracy: 8);   //  84 m away  → nothing
  gps.emit(lat, lng, accuracy: 8);   //  31 m away  → IGNITES
  gps.emit(lat, lng, accuracy: 8);   //  12 m away  → stays quiet
  gps.emit(lat, lng, accuracy: 90);  // junk fix    → dropped

  expect(game.unlocked, ['futala_lake']);
});

Keep the distance maths as plain functions over latitude and longitude, with no plugin and no async, and every edge case above costs nothing to test. The same fake doubles as a GPS joystick in a development build, which is how you demo the app indoors without pretending to walk.

Shipping without a store

Ember is not going to Google Play, which removes more work than it adds. There is no data-safety declaration, no content rating, no review queue, and no store listing. It also removes the single biggest obstacle to background unlocks: Play requires a written justification and a demo video before it will approve ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION, and that review is why most side projects never ship the feature. Off-store, the only remaining limits are the operating system's own, so background unlocks become a purely technical decision whenever you want them.

ConcernOff-store answer
Android buildflutter build apk --release against your own keystore, installed directly. Build a per-ABI split only if the download size starts to matter.
Getting it onto phonesSend the APK, or use Firebase App Distribution for tester groups and update notifications. Neither needs a Play listing.
Maps API keyStill restrict it, to your package name plus your release keystore's SHA-1. Off-store keys leak the same way on-store keys do, and an unrestricted key in a shared APK is someone else's bill.
iOSThe expensive half. Real-device install needs a paid Apple developer account and TestFlight or an ad-hoc profile. Treat Android as the target and iOS as optional, since Flutter keeps the door open either way.
UpdatesNo auto-update off-store. Put a version string in the app and have it check a URL, or simply re-send the APK. Do not build an updater.
07

When a backend arrives

Not yet. But these two decisions are cheap now and expensive later.

Ember's data has a property most games would envy: unlocks only ever accumulate. A place never becomes un-visited, so two devices that disagree are merged by taking the union of both sets, and a write replayed twice is harmless if the document is keyed by place id. That removes the hardest part of offline sync entirely, and it is worth preserving deliberately rather than discovering by accident.

Shape

Store unlocks as a subcollection, one document per place, keyed by the place id: users/{uid}/unlocks/{placeId}. Not a map field on the user document, which forces a full read to check one place, sends every unlock to the same document, and grows toward the size ceiling. Place definitions themselves stay a bundled asset, since they are static content and paying to read them on every launch buys nothing.

Cheating

Say this out loud once: client GPS cannot be trusted, ever. A mock-location app reports any coordinate it likes, and app attestation does not help, because it proves the app is genuine without proving the reading inside it is. The goal is raising cost, not prevention.

Three mitigations, in descending order of value:

  • Make unlocks unwritable by the client. Reads are direct, writes go through one server function holding the real coordinates and doing the distance check itself. This alone defeats the "just set unlocked to true" attack, which is essentially all casual cheating.
  • Check implied speed between consecutive claims, using server timestamps rather than client ones. Two claims forty kilometres apart ninety seconds apart is a teleport, and rejecting it costs one extra stored field. This is the single best value-for-effort check in the whole system.
  • Read the mocked-location flag on Android and record it. It is trivially defeated, so treat it as a signal to review rather than grounds to reject, but it is free.

None of this is MVP work. It becomes worth building the day a token is worth something to somebody, and not one day earlier.

Settled, and worth writing down

  • Maps pricing is fine. Earlier in this project I said mobile map loads are free, then flagged that as unverified. It is now confirmed from the price list: the mobile Maps SDK line is a no-cost, unlimited item, and it does not count against any monthly allowance. The flat credit that ended in February 2025 was a different thing. The cost risk is not the map, it is the map identifier described above, plus the separate places and geocoding products, which do bill.
  • Do not source destinations from Google's places product. Its terms allow storing a place's identifier indefinitely but caching its coordinates for only thirty days, which a game built entirely from coordinates cannot comply with. Hand-curate the list, or take it from OpenStreetMap.
  • Permission order is enforced. Ask for while-in-use first and escalate only later. Requesting the wrong one first produces a rejected prompt you cannot show again.
  • If background unlocks ever happen, use the platform's own geofencing rather than a running GPS stream, and design around a cap of roughly twenty monitored regions on iOS by re-registering the nearest handful as the player moves. A continuous high-accuracy stream in the background drains a battery in hours and gets killed anyway. Note that the genre's own answer is different and better: read accumulated walking distance from the phone's health data instead of tracking location at all.
